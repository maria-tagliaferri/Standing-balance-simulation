Learning Ankle Exoskeleton Control for Reactive Standing Balance Through Neuromusculoskeletal Simulation

Maria T. TagliaferriInseung Kang

Department of Mechanical Engineering, Carnegie Mellon University

Abstract

Deficits in reactive balance can increase fall risk among older adults and individuals with neuromuscular impairments, motivating the need for interventions that improve balance recovery. Wearable robotic exoskeletons offer a promising approach to addressing this need by augmenting balance control. However, developing controllers that deliver appropriately timed and scaled assistance remains challenging, as evaluating each candidate strategy requires repeated exposure to destabilizing perturbations.

We propose a simulation-to-real framework that combines motion imitation and reinforcement learning to develop balance-assistive ankle exoskeleton control. A muscle-driven neuromusculoskeletal model was trained using a small set of motion-capture data from a single subject to reproduce kinematic and kinetic responses to backward-translation perturbations. This model served as a virtual human for training a reinforcement-learning exoskeleton controller. The resulting controller generated bilateral ankle assistance using only inertial sensor measurements, without explicit perturbation detection.

We implemented the controller on-board a bilateral ankle exoskeleton and evaluated it in five healthy subjects whose data were not used for training. Assistance increased the perturbation magnitude that subjects could withstand without stepping by 11.8% and reduced peak center-of-mass displacement and velocity by 13.5% and 28.2%, respectively. Individual benefits varied with baseline recovery strategy and body mass. These results indicate that a single virtual human can yield transferable balance assistance, and that more diverse virtual humans could further improve its effectiveness.

Phase 1

Motion imitation in neuromusculoskeletal simulation

We first developed a virtual human capable of reproducing balance responses to backward-translation perturbations through motion imitation. A 10-degree-of-freedom musculoskeletal model with 18 muscles was trained using Soft Actor–Critic to track approximately 2 minutes of motion-capture data from a single subject.

The policy was rewarded for tracking recorded joint angles and joint moments, with additional terms that encouraged recovery toward the reference following large perturbation-induced deviations. It received no explicit information about perturbation onset, duration, or direction.

Phase 2

Training the exoskeleton policy

With the human policy frozen, an ankle exoskeleton was added to the model, and a second policy was trained to provide assistance. The exoskeleton policy used only signals available to the physical device: a 200 ms history of shank and foot angular velocities and its previous torque commands. It output a single bilateral torque command, with a maximum magnitude of 16 Nm.

The policy was rewarded for avoiding steps and reducing center-of-mass displacement and velocity during perturbation recovery. It received no explicit perturbation detection signal, and no assistance onset, timing, or magnitude was prescribed. Instead, the policy learned when and how much to assist, generating plantarflexion torque that peaked before the biological ankle-moment response and increased the simulated balance threshold by 4%.

Phase 3

Transfer to hardware

The trained policy was deployed on-board a bilateral ankle exoskeleton and used only on-board inertial sensor measurements, without explicit perturbation detection or subject-specific tuning. It was evaluated in five healthy participants whose data were not used for training. Each participant's balance threshold was measured with and without exoskeleton assistance.

What the results show

To our knowledge, this work is the first to train a balance-assistive ankle exoskeleton controller in neuromusculoskeletal simulation and deploy it on a physical device. Unlike prior balance-assistive controllers that deliver predefined torque profiles at prescribed delays or rely on a separate perturbation-detection stage, the learned policy generated assistance directly from real-time shank and foot angular velocities. It delivered plantarflexion torque that peaked before the biological ankle-moment response, and adjusted assistance magnitude and duration to the specific perturbation.

The simulation reproduced key features of the hardware response, including assistance timing relative to the biological response and the reduction in biological ankle moment under assistance. However, it underestimated the threshold improvement (4% in simulation vs. 11.8% on hardware), and on hardware the assistance began earlier and reached a slightly higher peak torque. These differences highlight that the behavior and performance of simulation-based control depend on the fidelity of the virtual human and the device model.

The benefit of assistance also depended on the user. Participants who relied more on the ankle during exo-off recovery gained the most, suggesting that the effectiveness of ankle assistance depends partly on how individuals naturally regulate balance. The smaller gains in heavier participants may reflect the lower relative assistance provided by the fixed 16 Nm torque limit.

Conclusion

This work trained a balance-assistive ankle exoskeleton controller entirely in neuromusculoskeletal simulation and deployed it on hardware without subject-specific tuning. In five participants not used for training, the learned assistance increased the balance threshold and reduced peak center-of-mass displacement and velocity during backward-translation perturbations. The findings of this study motivate extending this framework to more diverse virtual human populations and broader perturbation conditions, so that the learned assistance accounts for the range of body sizes and recovery strategies seen across users.
