Learning Ankle Exoskeleton Control for Reactive Standing Balance Through Neuromusculoskeletal Simulation

Maria T. TagliaferriInseung Kang

Department of Mechanical Engineering, Carnegie Mellon University

Paper arXiv Videos BibTeX

Abstract

Deficits in reactive balance can increase fall risk among older adults and individuals with neuromuscular impairments, motivating the need for interventions that improve balance recovery. Wearable robotic exoskeletons offer a promising approach to addressing this need by augmenting balance control. However, developing controllers that deliver appropriately timed and scaled assistance remains challenging, as evaluating each candidate strategy requires repeated exposure to destabilizing perturbations.

We propose a simulation-to-real framework that combines motion imitation and reinforcement learning to develop balance-assistive ankle exoskeleton control. A muscle-driven neuromusculoskeletal model was trained using a small set of motion-capture data from a single subject to reproduce kinematic and kinetic responses to backward-translation perturbations. This model served as a virtual human for training a reinforcement-learning exoskeleton controller. The resulting controller generated bilateral ankle assistance using only inertial sensor measurements, without explicit perturbation detection.

We implemented the controller on-board a bilateral ankle exoskeleton and evaluated it in five healthy subjects whose data were not used for training. Assistance increased the perturbation magnitude that subjects could withstand without stepping by 11.8% and reduced peak center-of-mass displacement and velocity by 13.5% and 28.2%, respectively. Individual benefits varied with baseline recovery strategy and body mass. These results indicate that a single virtual human can yield transferable balance assistance, and that more diverse virtual humans could further improve its effectiveness.

Simulation-to-real framework

A virtual human learns to respond to perturbations like a real one, then an exoskeleton policy learns to help it, and that policy runs directly on the device.

Stage 1

Motion imitation

A muscle-driven model with 18 muscles is trained with Soft Actor–Critic to track about 2 minutes of motion-capture data from one subject, reproducing joint angles and moments during backward belt translations.

Stage 2

Exoskeleton policy

With the human policy frozen, an exoskeleton policy learns ankle torque from a 200 ms history of shank and foot angular velocity. Timing and magnitude are not prescribed; they emerge from a stability-focused reward.

Deployment

On-board control

The policy runs on a bilateral ankle exoskeleton (up to 16 Nm) using only its inertial sensors, with no perturbation detection and no subject-specific tuning.
